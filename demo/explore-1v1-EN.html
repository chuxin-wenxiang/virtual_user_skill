Hotel Room Type Selection — User Decision Anxiety

Virtual User Exploration Mode · 1v1 In-depth Interview Insights

📅 2026-06-16 🔍 Corpus Match: Top 20 from 54,631 scenarios 👥 Virtual Users Interviewed: 2

Research Background

This exploration focused on a core product experience question: What makes the platform's hotel users hesitate most when choosing a room type?

Using the Virtual User Skill's exploration mode, we retrieved 20 highly relevant scenarios from a corpus of 54,631 real user research entries, then constructed two differentiated personas representing two typical hesitation paths: "information overload paralysis" and "upgrade value uncertainty."

Method: Semantic vector retrieval (text2vec-base-chinese, 768d) → persona generation → 1v1 semi-structured in-depth interview

Interview 1: The Information Architecture Trap

C
Carol · Established Middle Class
Age 37 Married, 1 child (primary school) ¥30k/month Fashion industry Family traveler
Researcher
Tell me about your most recent experience choosing a room type on the platform.
Carol
Last time I was booking a resort in Sanya for my family. Clicked in and saw seven or eight room types — "Superior Sea View," "Deluxe Ocean Suite," "Premium Family Room," "Select Garden Twin"… The names were fancier than the last, but I couldn't tell what actually differed between them.
Researcher
Was it just the names that confused you, or other information too?
Carol
Both. Once you tap in, it gets worse. Under a single room type there are multiple "packages" — with breakfast, without breakfast, free cancellation until X date, non-refundable. Prices from ¥400 to ¥900 all listed together. I spent ages scrolling through just one room type. And there were six more waiting outside…
Researcher
What was the core source of your hesitation?
Carol
I know exactly what I need: a king bed with an extra bed option, breakfast included, free cancellation — because plans change with kids. But the options matching my criteria were buried among irrelevant ones. I had to eliminate them one by one. Spent half an hour still wondering if the ¥200 gap between "Deluxe" and "Superior" was worth it.
Researcher
Did you figure out what that ¥200 difference actually bought you?
Carol
Not really. The description said "larger area" without specifying how many square meters more. "Higher floor" without saying which floor. In the end I picked the pricier one, thinking "the expensive one can't be bad"… but that decision-making process felt terrible. Like being forced to choose blind.
Researcher
If you ranked your frustration points?
Carol
First, what actually differs between room types — fancy names but vague substance. Second, too many package combinations. Third, whether the price gap is justified — nobody explains clearly. Fourth, suitability for kids — extra bed, children's amenities — I have to dig through reviews myself.
"It's not that there's too much information — it's that too much of it is irrelevant to me."

🔑 Key Insight

Root Cause: Information architecture doesn't serve the decision path. Excessive options × abstract naming × no personalized filtering = inflated comparison time.

Underlying Need: Help me make a confident decision in 5 minutes, instead of spending 30 minutes placing a bet that "the expensive one is probably fine."

Interview 2: The Upgrade Value Black Box

X
Xiao Chen · Gen Z
Age 22 Single ¥20k/month Foreign trade Travels with partner / colleagues
Researcher
When does the hesitation start for you when picking a room type on the platform?
Xiao Chen
After I pick a hotel and tap into the room list — I notice the cheapest option and the next tier up are only about ¥100 apart. That's when my brain starts going: "I'm already spending this much, would another ¥100 make it noticeably better?" But the problem is I can't actually see what makes it better.
Researcher
How do you usually end up deciding?
Xiao Chen
If I'm traveling with my girlfriend, I lean toward the pricier one — don't want to ruin the mood over ¥100. For work trips with colleagues, I go cheap. But either way, I feel uneasy after deciding. Pick the expensive one and wonder if I got ripped off. Pick the cheap one and wonder if I'll regret it.
Researcher
Is that unease from insufficient information or unclear personal standards?
Xiao Chen
Insufficient information. My standards are crystal clear — clean, quiet, bed not too small. But neither "Standard Room" nor "Superior Room" lets me confirm these three things before I book. They write "comfortable king bed" — is that 1.5m or 1.8m? I've even checked in to find a "king bed" that was actually 1.2m.
Researcher
Beyond specs, what other information would help your decision?
Xiao Chen
I desperately want to see "reviews that specifically mention this room type". Like someone saying "the Superior is genuinely quieter than Standard" or "that extra ¥100 only gets you a bathtub I never used." This kind of peer feedback is infinitely more useful than the hotel's own description. But the review section is a catch-all for the entire property.
Researcher
If you could change only one thing?
Xiao Chen
When I'm torn between two room types, just tell me what the price difference buys. Pop a card: "+¥120 = Balcony (4m²) + High floor (15-20F) + Bathtub." One line and I can instantly judge if it's worth it.
"I'm not hesitating about the choice itself — I'm hesitating because I don't know what I'm choosing. I'm not afraid to spend money; I'm afraid of spending it and finding out it's no different from the cheap option."

🔑 Key Insight

Root Cause: Upgrade value is opaque. Small price gap × invisible difference × reviews not segmented by room type = post-decision anxiety.

Underlying Need: Every yuan spent should be clearly accounted for — give me instant, concrete feedback on "what my extra money buys."

Product Opportunity Areas

1
Instant Room Type Comparison Card — When a user hesitates between two room types, surface a difference summary: "+¥X = specific gains." Make implicit differences explicit.
2
Profile-based "Relevant to Me" Filter — Auto-collapse irrelevant room types based on travel companions and trip purpose. Surface matching options first (e.g., family travelers see only extra-bed-capable rooms).
3
Reviews Segmented by Room Type — Under each room type, show "What people who stayed in this room said" instead of whole-property review aggregation. Let peer feedback directly serve room-level decisions.
4
Mandatory Key Parameter Transparency — Room area (m²), bed width (cm), floor range, window type (window/bay window/none) as structured fields with enforced display.

Methodology

Data Foundation: 54,631 real user research scenarios (NPS feedback + in-depth interviews), Fernet encrypted storage.

Retrieval Method: text2vec-base-chinese (768-dim) semantic vector search, cosine similarity Top-20, highest match score 0.797.

Persona Generation: Constructed from retrieved scenarios' background info, pain points, and needs fields, ensuring demographic and behavioral diversity.

Interview Method: Semi-structured 1v1 in-depth interview with first-person role-play constrained by persona context.

Limitations: Virtual users are projected from historical corpus rather than real-time humans. Exploratory findings require quantitative validation (recommend PMF mode with 20+ participants).
